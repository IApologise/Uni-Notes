Factoring is used whenever we want to simplify or merge two
clauses in a straightforward way where it couldn't be otherwise
through normal resolution steps if we also wish to preserve the
freedom of variables along the way.

For example, let's say we got {{p(x), p(y)}, {-p(u), -p(v)}}.
We could do it the normal way like this (this won't work):
1. p(x) V p(y)
2. -p(u) V -p(v)
3. p(y') V -p(v') by resolving 1 and 2 with x\u
4. p(x') V p(y'') by resolving 1 and 3 with u\y'
5. -p(u') V -p(v'') by resolving 2 and 3 with y\v'
6. p(y''') V -p(v''') by resolving 4 and 5 with x'\u'
7. ...
As you can see, nothing changes and we are still stuck.
This remains regardless of the combinations that we try.

Also, remember that each variable is bound by a different universal
quantifier, hence the reason why we denote those new variables through
' symbol like y into y' (since it was not substituted for nor changed).

To fix this we factor, meaning that we create a copy and apply a
substitution on it. This way we can get p(x') by factoring 1 with
x\y. So, let's see how it works in the previous example clause set:
1. p(x) V p(y)
2. -p(u) V -p(v)
3. p(x') by factoring 1 with x\y
4. -p(u') by factoring 2 with u\v
5. Ø by resolving 3 and 4 with x'\u'
