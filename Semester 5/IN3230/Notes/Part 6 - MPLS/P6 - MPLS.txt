--- Introduction to MPLS ---
MPLS stands for MultiProtocol Label Switching which is a way of forwarding packets using some kind of labels
instead of IP addresses. This is useful for when the router has decided that a certain path along the
network should be taken and the necessary resources have already been allocated for it. The labels then guide
the packet along that path since that isn't something IP can do on its own. MPLS is between Ethernet and IP
layers. And it directly addresses how during switching in the previous lecture a packet can take a certain
path it was given by the router and how those resources can be allocated. This means that instead of routers
and other devices looking at their layer's relevant data, such as a router forwarding packets based on the
packet header's destination IP address, it instead looks at the MPLS's label and uses that to decide where
to forward the packet. It is typically used to inside an infrastructure that has MPLS supported where it
sits before the IP Header.

In addition to that, one may separate connections between services without creating separate physical
networks by simply applying separate MPLS labels to each service which is what the internet providers do
to different customers, be it usual homes with their own home routers or big companies with multiple. It
allows for easy Network Consolidation which is the merging previously separate networks into a shared one,
including the possibility to connect two distant offices like in Oslo and in Trondheim into a WAN via its
own MPLS VPN, though the limitations and rules for those previously separate networks may still apply. And
it also allows for Network Segmentation which is splitting the network into smaller parts. With MPLS we get
lots of control over what can and can't be shared, and we also may impose limitations such as throughput,
latency, availability, packet loss, and jitter all of which can be capped and limited. All this is determined
in the SLA agreement between you and the service provider encoded into the MPLS labels used within the
provider's routing infrastructure.



--- Core MPLS & Its Domain ---
MPLS is a small additional header which as mentioned before encapsulates the IP layer such that its rules
are applied to the router first bypassing the IP routing. MPLS Headers do not make sense to routers that
do not support MPLS protocol, and home routers don't use those mainly due to trust issues between the
customer and the provider. As well as due to possible maintenance that the provider might need to do, and I
wouldn't want some IT guy come whenever into my house possibly several times a day to do some "IT stuff".
So, we distinguish between 4 types of routers:
1. Customer (C) - Those are the customer's routers used inside their infrastructure/domain which they have
   full access to and full control over.
2. Customer Edge (CE) - CEs are also part C's routing infrastructure, the only difference is that CEs can
   also forward packets to PEs since those are directly connected to each other.
3. Provider Edge (PE) - These are the provider's routers which are supposed to take the incoming packets
   from CEs and inject the appropriate MPLS Headers, then forward it to the P router. In case the packet was
   received from P it must strip the MPLS Header instead and then forward it to the CE. Those two operations
   are called Push and PoP (no typo here) for Imposition and Disposition respectively.
4. Provider (P) - These are provider's routers which forward packets based on their MPLS Header labels.
   They also switch the MPLS label's values on each hop. It could also perform the PE's job and also route
   to other PEs, not just Ps, but it depends on the topology. As long as it works with MPLS and has a
   direct connection to a CE it counts as a PE.
Also, both PEs and Ps are part of the Label Switching Routing (LSR) where Ps are sometimes called Core
Routers instead. Inside this LSR we have the MPLS Domain. Outside LSR there are no MPLS Headers.

Each P has a table on what to do with the MPLS label based on the values it reads. Specifically, once it
reads the label, its value determines which outgoing channel should the packet be forwarded through refered
to as Signaling, and what should the label's value be replaced with for the next router refered to as
Forwarding (quite counter intuitive naming at first glance, but I guess it just depends on perspective).
To know which label the router should swap to, a system called Label Distribution Protocol (LDP) is used.
The idea is to have routers exchange labeling tables with their neighbours. Labels represent a range of
destionation IP Addresses that the forwarding mapping applies to, and as we know labels are set by PEs
based on which CE it came from. Technically speaking, labels are just identifiers of different Forwarding
Equivalence Classes (FECs) which is what actually determines how packets are to be forwarded. So, what PEs
actually do is perform IP-routing for a whole path to be followed, i.e. FEC, and give that path an ID, i.e.
label. Each router now using their tables and the labels know where the packet should go next.

I have mentioned that MPLS Headers contain Labels, but they also contain Time To Live (TTL), Class of Service
(COS, aka. EXP), and Bottom of Stack (S). Checkout "L6 - MPLS Header.png" for location and size in bits,
though all in all, the header is 4B (bytes) or 32b (bits) in size. Bottom of Stack tells us whether there
are more than just one MPLS Headers after/before each other (direction not specified), though we only need 2.
First one is for forwarding between the Ps, and the second one is for when PEs strip the first one. The
second MPLS tells the outgoing PE some details on what additional things it should do to the packet before
it sends it to a CE, for instance in case CE uses a VPN. As for COS that comes later since it isn't part of
the Core MPLS (back when it was introduced).



--- Modern Extended Version of MPLS ---
Core MPLS does not provide bandwidth reservation, congestion avoidance, guaranteed latency, guaranteed packet
loss, automatic protection against every failure, cryptographic confidentiality, nor path selection (routing
is the job of a router, MPLS just enforces the use of the path). However, modern MPLS can do some of that
stuff compared to the Core MPLS which could only do Forwarding and Signaling. Check out "P6 - MPLS
Mechanisms.png" to see what was added (things highlighted yellow is what we covered thus far).

A router's job thus far can be split into 4 parts, a nice model to use is this:
1. Routing Information Base (RIB) which is the part that creates a table determening the next hop for a
   packet to go through using its table made up of its own telemetry and shared tables of other routers.
2. Forwarding Information Base (FIB) is the part that uses RIB's table to forward the packet (next hop).
3. Label Information Base (LIB) is where MPLS comes in. It contains the label-related information such as
   label to FEC mappings learned via LDP mechanism. Though LDP takes place after the RIB's IGP is done since
   it relies on PE's routing first. IGP is a mechanism that creates these routing tables.
4. MPLS Forwarding Infrastructure (MFI) is much like FIB is for RIB. We simply take a look up our label in
   our LIB to know what to do with it, such as Swap or PoP the label, or perform some sort of action.
   Coincidentally, that is also where MPLS comes in, its usage to be more precise.
Later we will introduce TED and maybe some more stuff, but all that will be explained later...

Now then, let's talk about how modern MPLS extends the Core MPLS by introducing the following mechanisms:
1. Quality of Service (QoS) is when a PE looks into the packet's information such as protocol, port, etc.
   to add extra constraints to the routing process. For instance, video conferencing requires high bandwidth,
   low delay, and low packet loss for a smooth show. It classifies the needs and inserts an identifier for
   that class in the COS section of the MPLS Header. The routers read the identifier and use it to get the
   class description which they have in their local database. This type of marking is called traffic-class
   (TC). This also allows for implementing stuff like queueing priorities useful during congestion, provide
   a weighted share of transmission capacity, and more. As a concrete example, imagine a congested network
   and packets one of which is a file download and the other is a video conference. File download isn't
   crucial, but a video conference's every packet loss and delay is very noticeable and frustrating. With
   MPLS we can prioritize video conferences over file downloads fixing the video conference lagging issue
   at the cost of slowing down that someone's file download.

2. Traffic Engineering (TE) adds goals and constraints that may influence and control the paths that the
   packets may take. An example of its usage would be to avoid congested links even though it might take
   more hops, or a particular router/link due to high packet loss telemetry at that point. It is also
   what may determine a specific path the packet takes called tunneling, or an altenrative path in case
   of some unforseen failure. Core MPLS simply follows the predetermined route generated by the IGP. TE
   on the other hand can calculate own routes independently of the IGP, meaning it does not rely on RIB.
   
   Since it creates a route it first needs to learn of the MPLS Domain topology. Router's original OSPF and
   IS-IS already distribute the topology information. However, we need more information such as bandwidth,
   latency, etc. all of which the modified OSPF-TE and IS-IS-TE provide. These aren't anything new, they
   simply share and carry extra telemetry along with the original topology related information as they
   usually do. All of this telemetry for TE are stored in the router's TE-Database (TED), excluding what
   can already be found in RIB and LIB, simply to avoid duplicate data storage.
   
   Afterwards it must compute a path satisfying the tunnel's requirements. Traditionally routers have been
   using Shortest Path First (SPF) computed via Dijkstra's Search Algorithm. However, using TED we may
   impose additional contraints, i.e. compute paths via CSPF (Constrained SPF) as a way to filter out or
   change the cost of some paths, though it now may no longer guarantee an eligible path under the given
   constraints if we just filter it out (and we often do). Also, we perform Fast ReRoute (FRR) too which
   is basically calculating an alternative path in case the current tunnel suddenly fails.
   
   Once the path has been computed we need to signal the path to the routers on that path, establish
   forwarding state, and reserve resources. Later the tunnel's conditions might change too, so we also need
   to update the relevant routers accordingly. Establishing a path is done via RSVP-TE mechanism which is an
   extension of the Resource ReserVation Protocol (RSVP). It may allocate labels and distribute them across
   the LSP, signal to reserve resources, and modify the existing establishment. The signaling is made up of
   two messages, the "PATH" and the "RESV" which are sent to the routers along the path. "PATH" encodes the
   necessary information to establish the tunnel such as label information, and the "RESV" is for resource
   reservation. The implementations of "PATH" and "RESV" may differ though... RSVP-TE is similar to LDP,
   but LDP establishes label mappings for FECs using IGP derived paths, whilst RSVP-TE uses CSPF's routes.

3. Operations, Administration, and Maintenance (OAM), as well as Management Information Bases (MIBs), are
   both used together. Note that I will not explain how it works. OMA is about monitoring, testing, and
   maintaining the network. OAM can be used to monitor and verify MPLS paths, tunnels and services, and help
   detect faults. For example, to verify that the MPLS LSP between the two PEs is functioning. MIB contains
   information about a network device, which systems can access and analyze through protocols such as SNMP.
   It helps OAM to find which device is causing problems and where.



--- Virtual Private Networks ---
Virtual Private Networks (VPNs) are private networks (LANs) which are as if connected by some private router.
Basically we connect two LANs between the two distant places logically using encryption only the two of
these networks may understand. Don't confuse it with VLAN which splits a single LAN into multiple distinct
ones logically, both still are technically inside one and the same LAN but the router prohibits them from
communicating to one another (I think?). VPNs are for when you don't want to build a kilometer length
infrastructure yourself to connect the two distant places and would rather just use the provider's existing
one (imagine saying no to that).

The client may decide how much the provider interferes:
1. Layer-2 VPN - The provider transports the Ethernet Headers + any additional payload. This allows the two
   LANs to communicate as if they are in a single one. The routing is still the client's responsibility.
   The way this data is transported is done in two ways:
   A. Virtual Private Wire Service (VPWS) - This one provides the Point-To-Point connectivity between two
      endpoints. We basically translate MAC of PE1 to the MAC of CE2 which is connected to PE2 & vice versa.
      This limits the number of LANs that can be connected to just 2 though since there is no way to
      distinguish whether MAC coming from CE1 should go to CE2 or CE3 for instance. There is a way around
      that but I'd rather not go down the rabbit hole. AToM is how we refer to this strategy in general
      which is when we use MPLS and don't use the CE's MAC Address that the PE gets, just forward it.
   
   B. Virtual Private LAN Service (VPLS) - This provides multipoint Ethernet connectivity between multiple
      CEs, which basically is emulating that all the sides are connected by as if an Ethernet LAN or a
      broadcast domain. PEs learn of the MAC Addresses and maintain MAC forwarding tables and learn of
      each other's CE's MAC Addresses by sharing and via unicasts.

2. Layer-3 VPN - The provider does everything that Layer-2 VPN does and is also responsible for routing too
   such as exchanging the routing tables between the two client's LANs. The PEs on either side learn of the
   CE's topologies and other routing information and share that information with the other side's PE. CEs
   share that information via BGP or OSPF protocols (idk what those are). MultiProtocol BGP (MP-BGP) is what
   is used between the PEs to share the info mentioned before. Virtual Routing and Forwarding (VRF) allows
   PEs to maintain the separate Layer-3 VPNs between the separate customers and their CEs. It works by
   adding an RD which is a prefix and an identifier added on top of IPv4/IPv6 Addresses, then the RTs are
   used to actually distribute the data related to those connected PEs sharing the same VPN. That is where
   the second, inner MPLS Header is used, it contains the label of the VPN (their values are chosen to not
   collide with the forwarding labels).
