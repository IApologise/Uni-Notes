--- Fragmentation & Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU)---
Before back in the 70's or so when the internet was being developed there was fragmentation in the IP layer for
when the Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU) was too small. Today however it should not be used since there were lots
of problems which slowed everything down substantially. A fragmented packet must be reconstructed at the
destination router, but what if a fragment was lost? In that case it must wait for a long time in vain, and it
was also adding unnecessarily complex logic.

To avoid fragmentation people would always set the flag to 0 also known as the "don't fragment" flag. This would
mean that the packet would be dropped instead. With PMTUD the router would then send an ICMP error message to the
client whose packet was dropped, but this would often be filtered out by firewalls & dropped by other routers.
For that reason a better solution is to do what's called Packetization Layer PMTUD or PLPMTUD for short. A very
long name which also became a meme... Moving on, the way it would work is we simply start out with a reasonable
packet size, and if it gets dropped we reduce the size. If at some point we get an ACK then we increase the size.
Though please note that I am not sure whether the size is being reduced after a packet loss or if we simply retry
with the highest MTU we have so far. The problem with this is the time, as well as the inconsistency since the
packet can be dropped for many reasons such as congestion control, firewalls, etc. And it also creates traffic.
Even the path itself may change which would cause us to essentially restart our probing.



--- TCP Assumptions & Them Getting Broken ---
TCP creates a specific order and sends data in a specific way to then organize it & more (I don't really remember
that now). However, TCP assumes that during packet travels there is in general little re-ordering and little loss
of data, i.e. it works fastest when there is no re-ordering and no packet loss.

The problem with all this is that routers, which do not have enough data about the packets they are receiving,
can't usually make good decisions and are likely to slow down upper protocols because of that. For example to
ensure fairness & more reliable transmission the router might alternate the packets that are sent between similar
paths which are likely to create re-ordering for TCP, thus likely actually slowing everything down.

To not slow everything down the router might inspect the upper layer to make a per-connection distribution, i.e.
connection A's packets go via router X, and connection B's packets go via router Y, where both reach destination D.
However, the problem is that now the router is also working with the upper layer meant for ES-ES and no ISes.
This creates more overhead, layering starts to make less sense, and needs special treatment per each upper layer
protocol such as TCP, UDP, QUIC, SCTP, etc. The whole point of IP was to stay neutral, yet that is not what the
routers who the IP is meant for are doing...



--- Non-Technical Reasons for Bloat & Bad Design ---
Notice how there are so many parts in the IP header that are often not used or ignored, and not just the IP
header but on many layers in fact. That is because the big guys can't agree on how the systems should work and
want others to adapt to their own designs instead of adapting themselves. Because of that often in the meetings
peolpe would say what they want/need and what must be added to make the communication work with their routing
and systems. So if A demands X to work, and B demands Y to do the same, then why not add both X and Y. Though in
time the companies changed their ways and unused bits got adapted for other purposes, with backwards compatability
for used systems taken into the consideration...
