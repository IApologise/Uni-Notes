--- How Mailing Works ---
This is something I've never explained before, but basically whenever you hit send on an email our device has an
application generally refered to as the User Agent (UA) which performs the underlying logic by formatting and
sending the message to the next Mail Transfer Agent (MTA) which is its own end-system device. The MTA then
attempts to send the message further to the next MTA, but if it isn't available at the time the mail data is
simply stored and the MTA retries constantly (for hours/days) until it is. Finally the mail reaches the
recipient's Mail Delivery Agent (MDA) which waits until the target UA becomes available to receive the mail.
Note that the MTA and MDA have their own domain name types. DNS name has "NS", IP has "A", website server has
"ORIGIN", and mail has "MX".

Also, the application-layer protocol used to send mails from the UA and forward them between the MTAs up to the
MDA is called SMTP which stands for Simple Mailing Transfer Protocol, however to receive one from the MDA the
IMAP and POP3 are used. Though POP3 is old and such, so it's just IMAP now. IMAP has a port 143 and 993 if over
TLS, as for SMTP it is 25 and 587 if over TLS, though for some reason MTA-to-MTA forwarding uses "opportunistic"
TLS instead, meaning that TLS is used only if bots sides support it, otherwise it is sent without TLS, i.e. just
plain text instead, even though there is TLS between the UA and the MTA, which does request, but does not
guarantee that there is TLS along the whole mail travel path.

Note that technically the UAs could perform the MTA's jobs, but it if one would close the UA or shutdown their
devices after they hit sent whilst the recipient's UA is not available (e.g. not connected to network) then the
mail contents would be lost and no mail would be actually sent after the user hit the "send" button. A nice
addition is also that logic and security can be separated, all into a single server, which in my case is most
likely my ISPs local MTA. That local MTA also has a good reputation, so it is more likely to not get blocked
by other foreign MTAs for security reasons. The MDA is simply an MTA that does not forward data outwards, only
waits for data to come from outside and forwards it to the recipient UA once available. All in all, the main
purpose of MTAs and MDAs is to hold the mailed data until the recipient becomes is available such that the
sender might just hit "send" and exit from the system/app/service.

Often times there are only 2 MTAs involved (and always 1 MDA), those MTAs are sender's UA -> sender's local
ISP's MTA -> recipient's local ISP's MTA -> recipient's local ISP's MDA -> recipient's UA. If you are using
something like "@gmail.com" then it is google's MTA's and MDA's that are used instead of the ISPs. All mailing
systems have their own MTAs and MDAs which hold the data and forward it once it is ready to be received. So
those MTAs and MDAs don't have to be necessarily in the same network or physically close to the UA, other MTAs,
and other MDAs. However, corporations like to have it in their DMZs under the same firewall for convenience
for when they want their own mailing systems such as the UiO's "@uio.no", which has several MTAs for when you
want a specific branch such as "@ifi.uio.no", or "@jus.uio.no", etc.

Mailed data consists of 3 parts, MTA traversing history, Header, and Body. As the mailed data travels between
the MTAs each one adds itself to the path telling us who the MTA received the mailed data from as well as the
who the current MTA which received the mail data is. It includes the IPv6 address of the MTAs, connection type
(such as TLS) the domain name of the MTAs, and the date with time and the time zone (UTC). The log may also
include the machine's operatin system, ESMTP id, and more (details here aren't that important). The Header
includes info on whether it is cypher/plain text, charset such as UTF-8, date & time (and UTC) the mail was
written, sender and receiver mail names/domains, message title, and the addons used such as MIME v1.0. The Body
is the message itself minus the title since that is in the Header section, and that's it.

ESMTP is the extends the security of SMTP by adding authentication, unicode encoding, and secure transport where
the MTPs may establish TLS handshake (but first need to request it and list the supported connections). Not sure
if that may cause a downgrade attack though. As for MIME, that is the addon that allows the support of more
characters such as "æ", "å", "ø", etc. as well as the inclusion of png files and other types of encoding by
specifying boundaries in the Header which are used in the Body section.



--- Security & Attacking Tricks ---
Phishing is the most obvious attack that will always work as long as humans can be fooled (or make mistakes)
by means which grant unauthorized access, such as links. Some domain links are obvious, some have to be looked
at closer, and some are evil and use similar letters and phrases or typos such as the cyrillic "а" instead of
the english one "a" which looks the same but have different IDs which to a computer look different. Though this
usage of simillar letters is often pointed out by browsers today. Some domains may want to extract some info
from you such as your password, and a simple link might not give enough access so usually they might lead you to
a website that is made to be identical to the original from the human's point of view, so that when they enter
their passwords the victims won't notice anything wrong at first. However, with DNS poisoning one may use the
real link to the original website which will redirect to the attacker's one instead.

Privacy Breach is the tracking of when and where the mail was opened. Mails that utilize HTML such as .png images
with "src=https://..." force the mailing system to download the image from that website to show it to you. This
means that once you open a mail the website image gets downloaded and gets to know the IP address (your device's
approximate location). Not only that, each mail sent might utilize a different link which downloads the same
.png image, but because the links differ the sender can know exactly which user that it sent mail to has opened
it and when. This addition is typically called a "Token" represented as "...&auth=hash[e5be3db9]" which as you
can see contains a 64 bit hexadecimal code as identifier. To counter this simply turn off image loading, but
that is a quirk of HTML, who knows where else this may be used.

Next is SPAM (with all caps for some reason). The reason people spam mails is because it is cheap and easy, and
the companies don't risk anything other than their unauthenticated emails with no access to anything. Mails may
travel via foreign MTAs (not just google's MTAs if you use gmail for example). Those free, open to public MTAs
are called Open Relays, but one may restrict to accept mails only from certain types of domains which is how it
mostly works today, e.g. @gmail.com allows to take @uio.no's mails and vice versa, but not @spammer.com's. This
means that all MTAs have a sort of whitelist of domains they trust and are willing to receive data from. In
addition to that and because of spoofing, getting blamed, use of own resources for forwarding, hiding attacker's
IPs, spamming, etc. these Open Relays are very strict nowadays as well. So, MTAs are required to authenticate.
The whitelisting is called Sender Policy Framework (SPF) and you can find which MTAs accept from which mails
from the domain's DNS. For examples check the screenshots. Of course like with everything we got signatures
Domain Keys Identified Mail (DKIMs) which the domain's DNS has a public key for which is used to verify the
mail's contents. Lastly we got Domain Based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (DMARC) which
is basically answering the "what happens if SPF or DKIM is missing?" question. Should we ignore and forward?
Maybe reject and drop it? Also, it adds the check which verifies the "from:" found in SPF with the "from:"
found in the mail's header (I believe).

To sum it all up, nowadays the MTAs are wary of who the mails are coming from and must ask the MTAs to authorize
themselves before their messages may be accepted by the receiving MTAs. The three requirements which are used
to determine valid authentication are: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. Note that all of that doesn't prevent spam emails,
it just prevents unknown ones, i.e. unauthenticated ones. Though mailing systems still assume that users don't
want any spam and so they inspect the mail's contents for some patterns, filter out unsubscribed ones, and there
even is a "spam score" which adds/deducts points for how suspicious it is as a spam mail.

Note that STARTTLS only works between the MTAs, each MTA decrypts it, reads data, encrypts it back, and then
forward to the next MTA. But what if an attacker forced the STARTTLS to downgrade into plain text? That is where
S/MIME and PGP come in meant for end-to-end protection. S/MIME encrypts data and signs them using the email's
contents. It builds on top of MIME which means that the data travels as MIME parts. And since signatures are
involved it relies on the certificates issued by the CA, same as with HTTPS. Though verifying a person instead
of a website with certificates is arguably harder. Also, only the mail's Body section is the only one protected
here since the rest needs to be used for authorization among other things during travel between the MTAs, and it
also makes the key management a hassle. Though this is mostly solved by the enterprises that hvae their own CAs.
Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) is an alternative to the S/MIME. Here each mail address has its own certificates, and
others may choose sign to show that they trust it. That way a huge line of signatures is made which might
contain the signature of someone you trust when you receive a mail from an unknown source. Though Due to some
other difficulties and disadvantages the PGP is only used in small communities, and it also has vulnerabilities.
