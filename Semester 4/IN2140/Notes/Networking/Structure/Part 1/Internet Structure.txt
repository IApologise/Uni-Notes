--- Internet ---
Internet is split into 2 parts, end systems (ES) and intermediate systems (IS). ES are personal computers, phones,
printers, traffic lights, weather stations, etc. It is basically anything that doesn't re-send the same request
into other, further systems. So, unlike routers, switches, firewalls, etc. which do re-send the same requests and
data which makes them the intermediary systems. These intermediary systems is what is the internet, which is where
the data travels to get from one end system to another, like sending a photo from your computer to your phone, even
if they are close physically, but even then if there is a shortcut then the internet will make it (probably). In
fact, these intermediary steps are sometimes clustered closely together, which means that they are in the same subnet having a local IP address and such (i.e. LAN), and these clusters are called nets. So internet is basically
an inter communication between these nets. These nets are uncontrolled in size, like my appartment having a few
devices here and there, whilst IFI at UiO has thousands of computers students can access simultaneously and more.

Note that if the two devices are on the same LAN, it does not travel through the internet but to your switch,
which is most likely your home router (it has switch included in it) and then straight to the target device within
that LAN of yours (because it made a shortcut unless a LAN IP was used directly at which point it couldn't have
gone through the whole internet in the first place).



--- Topologies ---
Topologies is basically how devices are connected to each other. Like how end-systems are connected to a switch
through ethernet cables, how switches and routers are connected to each other, how routers are connected to each
other across the internet, etc. This also includes hardware such as how CPU cores connect to each other or how
PCIe slots are connected to the motherboard. We've seen them in IN2120, but a reminder is nice, here goes:

Bus - all nodes share a single cable. It is simple and cheap, but it has terrible performance because a single
collision creates interference, so everyone must wait for everyone. That is why it is mostly historical now.

Radio (ad-hoc wireless) topology - devices connect directly to nearby ones via radio waves without. There is no
central device here, and the data simply hops between them to reach its destination. Kind of like radio towers or
bluetooth and such where no fixed topology pattern or infrastructure exists (and wireless of course).

Star - all nodes connect to a single central node. It is used in LAN networks which is where devices connect to a
central switch or to a router and such. It is easy to manage and a single device failing doesn't affect others,
unless it is the central one that everyone is connected to...

Tree - a hierarchy of stars, i.e. switches connecting to switches connecting to a root switch/router. This is how
larger LANs and campus networks are built, like UiO having building-level switches connecting up to a central
campus router. This is also how PCIe works on motherboards (components branch off a root hub).

Ring - devices form a circle, each connected only to its two neighbors. Used in some older WAN technologies (like
SONET fiber networks) because a signal can travel either direction around the ring, giving redundancy.

Full mesh - every device connects directly to every other device. No conflicts with others and due to a direct
connection also fast. Note that Wi-Fi even though it reaches each device it is more like a bus topology, thought
I needed to clarify that. The number of connections explodes as you add devices though (because mesh) which is
n(n - 1) / 2 links where n is the number of devices, so it is only practical for small numbers of high-importance
devices, like core internet routers between major ISPs, or a small number of CPU cores in hardware.

Hypercube - a clever compromise for when full mesh becomes impractical. In a hypercube, each part connects to
exactly log_2(n) neighboring devices (where n is the total number of parts), arranged so any part can reach any
other in at most log_2(n) hops. So 8 nodes need only 3 connections each instead of 7. This scales much better than
full mesh while keeping paths short, which is why supercomputers use it.

Torus - imagine a grid of nodes where the edges wrap around and connect to each other (like a donut shape). Each
node connects to its 4 neighbors plus the wraparound connections. Used in supercomputers because it gives uniform
latency between any two nodes without the bottleneck risk of a tree. This is what the AI said basically...

Fat tree - a tree where the links get progressively wider (more bandwidth) toward the root. This solves a
bottleneck problem with regular trees where many devices competing to send traffic upward overwhelm the root
links. This one is commonly used in data centers for example.

Anyways, you can check all of these topologies out in the "Net Topologies X.png" where X is a number in the series.
Note that in the third one (i.e. "Net Topologies 3.png") the top one is the Bus topology, and the bottom one is
the Radio topology. Also, "Net Topologies 1.png" and "Net Topologies 2.png" have duplicates between them.

That said, supercomputers often use the hypercube structure. Sometimes also fat tree, torus topology, or just full
mesh. However, when it comes to the communication between the CPU and RAM (not just in supercomputers) it must be
much faster, and therefore has its own topologies that are being used: "Hypertransport", "QuickPath Interconnect",
and "Ultra Path Interconnect". One of these uses full mesh because 4 CPU cores are mangeable, but when it is more than that then we use another one which has a hypercube like system. Basically, these topologies are not only found
in networking, but also inside a devices, data centers, and more... Basically wherever some stuff communicates with
some other stuff, and not just a single one.



--- Layers ---
Each net may have their own solution as to how to communicate between other nets and the choice might also depend
on their priorities (such as latency being more important than scaling, or reliability and such). Today there are 3
common communication structures where one of them clearly dominates the rest. Layers is the most common one, then
there is components, and lastly recursion. We know layers from IN2120 intercommunication "Internet.png". Note that
each one has their strengths and weaknesses, and to be honest I am not sure how these communication systems talk to
each other, and whether they even can. And if they do, maybe I misunderstand what it is that we do with them and
what it is that they actually are.

Anyways, note that the Layers structure is split into two types, "Layers - OSI.png" being the first where OSI is
short for Open Systems Interconnection, and "TCP/IP.png" being the second which is the same one used in IN2120.
Layers is a serialized stream of bits where the whole stream is split into parts by its functionality once read.
So the first layer is the first sequence of bits in a stream of bits that is read by some device. Each layer is
processed by some device or a software within a device. I.e. it simply does something, or it reads its layer and
either modifies it and sends it further, or strips it and sends the layers above to the next device/software.
All of this depends on what layer it actually is.

The layers are broken down as follows based on how they are split (shown in the OSI model):
1. Physical layer is where we convert Wi-Fi signals (electro magnetic waves of radio frequency), ethernet signals
   (electricity in wires), optic fiber cables (electro magnetic waves of infra-red frequency), and so on... into
   bits (0s & 1s). This one is handled by devices such as a Modem, NIC, Wi-Fi Chips, Ethernet Ports, etc. No data
   is being processed or used in any way yet.

2. Data link layer is where the actual data starts being processed. Who sent it, who should receive it, and check
   if it is acceptable (not too many errors). The data that is processed is the whole stream of bits that was
   received. However, this and the other layers ignore the data that isn't meant to be read by it (not that it is
   important for it...). The data that is ignored is the DATA part which is true for all the layers (except for
   first and last since the first simply modulates/demodulates stuff and last one simply doesn't ignore anything
   since that is who the last part this data is meant for). The whole bit stream for this layer looks like this:
   [SOF, HEADER, DATA, CHK SUM, EOF] where SOF is the pattern of bits that denotes message start and EOF the end,
   DATA are the bits that are to be sent further and processed by the layers above (i.e. the part that is not to
   be stripped by the current layer), and CHK SUM is the error correction data. HEADER is the current layer's
   (layer 2 in this case) specific data which is to be processed, like a source and destination MAC address.

   On this layer we use MAC (Medium Access Control, not MAC address) which is responsible for channel access and
   framing. What it is and how it is done depends on whether we use Wi-Fi, Ethernet, etc. But basically it
   determines whose requested data to send first when available. Within Wi-Fi we do it so that the waves don't
   collide and interfere with each other, and for Ethernet we can send data when the cable is empty and no other
   data is going through it. Framing also depends, but it helps determine message's start and end (as well as how
   and when it is to be sent and such), so that is why we need SOF and EOF. MAC also looks at HEADER to figure out
   which local machine (device in the same LAN) sent the message and who is meant to receive it (within that same
   LAN using the MAC address that it read in HEADER). Then, the Logical Link Control (LLC) analyzes for errors
   based on what it reads (the whole bitstream) and in CHK SUM where too many errors may lead to a withdrawal.
   It also multiplexes (i.e. regulates whose message goes to who). Basically, MAC is the bridge between layer 1 &
   2, and LLC is between layer 2 & 3. All of this is the data link layer which as the name implies links stuff,
   i.e. layer 2 is just linking layers 1 and 3.
   
   The whole stream of bits that the data link layer received is called a frame (this includes the DATA part too
   which is what it does not process). Others have their own names such as layer 1 referring to it as "bits", layer
   3 referring to what is left of the stream after layer 2 stripped its part as "packets", similarly layer 4 calls
   its "segments". Data Link Layer also has ARP and RARP which is how the MAC <-> IP translation is done, more on
   it in other notes though. Note that most of what is written here is a general clarification and not just the
   layer 2 itself. It is said so whenever it is or generalized as "this" or "current" layer.

3. Network layer takes in the DATA part which is what is left after layer 2 stripped its layer (all layers do
   something similar except for the first one, and the last one simply has nothing to strip). Data link layer is
   the bridge between layers 1 and 3, but the network layer is what actually finds the next node to send the data
   to (private/public IP addresses are used here). To do that it considers many paths (think graph theory) and
   chooses the best it found (if one exists). The path chosen isn't always straightforward, like it being a bad
   idea to send data through the route whose nodes frame capacity (even if just one) won't be able to handle the
   one you'll send. Though in reality it just has some table it uses for that and uses the default gateway if it
   couldn't find out where it should send the message to. In this layer we also get to use firewalls to decide
   whether to allow or block incoming traffic (just the IP checks, very simple since layers above aren't included
   yet, which entails ports from layer 4 and application data from layer 7).

4. Transport layer is the "end-to-end message stream" which has data on who the target process within the machine
   is, i.e. the port. It also maintains the connection between the processes, some more end-to-end error correction
   (idk what type it is), and it does some extra features to make layer 5's job easier. Like regulating sending
   speed (or not), deliver packets in order (or not), correct for errors (or not), limit packet size (or not), and
   whether we do want the connection to be maintaned and established (or not), and more. This includes reassembling
   the packets back into the correct order, because they may not arrive in the order they were sent in due to how
   global routing is done. However, the fragmentation (splitting up data into individual packets, sending them, and
   reading them) is done each time a message is sent or received between the routers (switches and modems just
   forward what is given to them). Note that the 4th Layer simply orders the received data back, and it is the 3rd
   Layer that splits the data up and then recombines the pieces (not necessarily ordered).

   So here we got TCP & UDP connection types and others as well. TCP, UDP, etc. all of these are separate protocols
   all of which have one thing in common: They all mention the source and destination ports. The ports point to a
   process within a machine (its ID), which is the address to the process within the machine should receive the
   payload. Ports such as SSH, HTTP, HTTPS, and others including temporary ones that your device's processes create
   and assigns dynamically are more of a layer 5-7 thin. Once a process receives the port it is the process that
   decides what to do with it and how, as well as whoever sent it deciding what to send. Concentions are what often
   ultimately decides what the port means (unless both the user and the server expect something else).

   The Layer 4 is more about getting the message to the device that is listening to this port, and not what will
   be done with it afterwards nor how it will be interpreted. Also, layer 4 is where we got Firewalls restricting
   ports and routers doing Port Forwarding (kinda like NAT and firewall for ports). Lastly, TCP orders the
   fragmented packets received (such is the protocol), whilst UDP does not (though often handled by the application
   itself to make sure you watch the video in an intended order).

5. Sessions layer is the establishment, maintenance, and termination of the connection between the end-systems. It
   is adding some data which tells for how long the established connection is to be managed, and what to do if it
   is temporarily lost. For example still being authenticated mid-transaction if the connection gets lost. Often
   you don't even notice it. Or like creating checkpoints to continue the download of a file. It also fixes the
   multi-tasking and forking problems too, like video and sound synchonization and such. It is basically the
   authorization, synchroniation, and recovery part of this whole communications part.

6. Presentation layer does the data translation (encoding and decoding). It translates the application into an
   acceptable format to be sent over the internet such as converting a UTF-8 into bits. It also handles the
   compression of this data for a more efficient sendoff (like with images). Serialization and deserialization
   are also a key part of it all. Here encryption and decryption is also done. Basically the secure and efficient
   translation of the Layer 7 (both ways).

   A bit of an important note: Port 443 also known as HTTPS is a convention that the browsers and servers have
   agreed to use. So whenever you try to connect to YouTube through a port 443 the browser will assume that you
   want an HTTPS connection (HTTP & TLS). HTTP is the layer 7 thing which is a protocol on how the DATA part must
   be formatted to be understood by the server (which is what the server expects once you do port 443 or 80). TLS
   is the layer 6 thing which is what the browser attempts to achieve at this layer once you used port 443.
   However, I could in theory have my servers listen on port 8080 for any HTTPS requests, or have my servers
   listen for HTTP on port 443 assuming browsers don't enforce TLS, but they often do and raise an error...

7. Application layer is where the data is applied by a website or whatever the process is (such as a minecraft
   server) and such. At this level the processes may analyze and recognize what the ports 80 (HTTP) mean and act
   accordingly (rather than just listening for one which is what Layer 4 grants us). We also get cookies :)
